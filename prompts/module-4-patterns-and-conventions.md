# MODULE 4 — Failure Pattern Library & UK Conventions

**Injection:** Select a maximum of 8 relevant patterns per call based on what you detect in the CV. Do not inject the whole library.

---

# PART 1 — THE 32 FAILURE PATTERNS

Each pattern includes: what it looks like, why it costs the candidate, and the exact fix.

---

## CATEGORY A — SEVEN-SECOND FAILURES

### A1. The Buried Lede
**Looks like:** Best achievement on page two, or in the final bullet of the final role.
**Why it costs:** Page two receives almost no attention in the first scan. Location beats content quality.
**Fix:** Move the single strongest piece of evidence into the summary line. Everything else can stay where it is.

### A2. The Empty Top Third
**Looks like:** A three-sentence summary containing only adjectives, occupying the area where eyes land first.
**Why it costs:** You are spending your most valuable real estate on zero information while 139 competitors do the same.
**Fix:** Replace with one sentence containing a specific number and a named thing you built or changed.

### A3. The Two-Column Trap
**Looks like:** Skills sidebar on the left, experience on the right.
**Why it costs:** Fights F-pattern reading and often breaks CV parsing. Multiple columns are explicitly associated with poor recruiter performance.
**Fix:** Single column. Always.

### A4. The Header Trap
**Looks like:** Contact details in a Word header or footer.
**Why it costs:** Many systems do not read headers and footers. The recruiter may be unable to contact you.
**Fix:** Put contact details in the body of the document, first line.

### A5. The Text Wall
**Looks like:** Paragraphs of prose describing each role instead of bullets.
**Why it costs:** Recruiters skip over text blocks in favour of scannable content. Your content is invisible.
**Fix:** Convert to bullets, maximum two lines each.

### A6. Missing Section Headers
**Looks like:** Roles and education run together with no clear labels.
**Why it costs:** A lack of section or job headers actively turns recruiters off, as does text that does not draw the eye down the page.
**Fix:** Left-aligned, bold, consistent headers: Summary, Experience, Projects, Education, Skills.

### A7. The Back-Loaded Bullet
**Looks like:** "Working as part of a small team, I was responsible for building the dashboard that increased conversion by 12%."
**Why it costs:** During vertical scanning only the first two or three words of each line are guaranteed to be read. Yours are "Working as part."
**Fix:** "Increased conversion 12% by building a dashboard that surfaced drop-off by channel."

### A8. The Four-Page CV
**Looks like:** Every role since 2016 with six bullets each.
**Why it costs:** Some recruiters reject an over-long CV outright, and padding is as visible as cramming.
**Fix:** Graduates: one page, two maximum. Experienced: two, three maximum. Cut oldest and least relevant first.

## CATEGORY B — EVIDENCE FAILURES

### B9. The Duty List
**Looks like:** "Responsible for managing the reporting process."
**Why it costs:** Describes the job, not the person. Interchangeable with everyone who held that title.
**Fix:** State what changed because you held it. Add the number.

### B10. The Unbaselined Percentage
**Looks like:** "Increased traffic by 300%."
**Why it costs:** Recruiters know a 300% rise from 10 visits is meaningless. Percentage claims without baselines are discounted heavily and damage credibility.
**Fix:** "Grew monthly traffic from 4,000 to 16,000 over five months."

### B11. The Suspiciously Round Number
**Looks like:** "Improved efficiency by 30%." "Reduced costs by 50%."
**Why it costs:** 73% of hiring managers believe they can spot AI-written CVs, often from suspiciously round statistics. Some report AI tools inventing metrics outright. A fake number is far more dangerous than a missing one, because it collapses the moment it is probed.
**Fix:** Use the real, awkward number. "Cut close cycle from 9 days to 6." Precision reads as truth.

### B12. The Unmeasurable Metric
**Looks like:** "Improved reporting efficiency." "Enhanced team productivity."
**Why it costs:** These are not measurable quantities. An interviewer asking "how did you measure that?" will expose it.
**Fix:** Convert to something countable: time, money, volume, error rate, percentage of a defined total.

### B13. The Assisted-With Pattern
**Looks like:** "Assisted with month-end close." "Helped with the migration." "Supported the team."
**Why it costs:** Signals presence, not ownership. Recruiters read it as "someone else did this."
**Fix:** State exactly which part you owned. If you genuinely only assisted, name the specific contribution.

### B14. The Outcome-Free Project
**Looks like:** "Built a machine learning model to predict customer churn."
**Why it costs:** No accuracy figure, no baseline, no business recommendation. Reads as a tutorial completed.
**Fix:** Add the metric, the baseline, and what you would do with the finding.

### B15. The Tutorial Dataset
**Looks like:** Titanic, Iris, Netflix ratings, Amazon reviews, MNIST, house prices from a Kaggle starter set.
**Why it costs:** Recruiters see these dozens of times per hiring cycle. They signal course completion, not capability.
**Fix:** Replace with self-sourced data. Government open data, a public API, scraped data, or your own exported data from a service you use.

### B16. The Invisible Scale
**Looks like:** "Managed a team." "Handled a budget." "Ran the process."
**Why it costs:** A team of 2 and a team of 30 are different jobs. Without scale the claim is unassessable.
**Fix:** Every leadership or ownership claim gets a number attached.

## CATEGORY C — AUTHENTICITY FAILURES (the 2026 category)

### C17. The Interchangeable Summary
**Looks like:** "Results-driven professional with a proven track record of delivering value in fast-paced environments."
**Why it costs:** Recruiters have read that exact sentence a thousand times and it tells them nothing about you.
**Fix:** Delete every adjective about yourself. Replace with one specific thing you did.

### C18. The Optimised Clone
**Looks like:** A CV that matches the job description almost word for word, perfectly formatted, every keyword present.
**Why it costs:** This is the defining 2026 problem. AI lets candidates tailor applications to fully match job descriptions — but the result is that applications appear well written and highly relevant while being increasingly similar, forcing employers to spend more time validating whether experience is genuine. Perfect optimisation now makes you indistinguishable.
**Fix:** Keep the keyword alignment, but add at least three details that could only come from your actual life. Odd numbers, specific tool versions, named constraints you worked around, things that went wrong.

### C19. Impersonal Phrasing
**Looks like:** "Leveraged cross-functional synergies to drive stakeholder alignment."
**Why it costs:** 51% of recruiters cite unnatural phrasing as the top tell for AI-written applications.
**Fix:** Write it as you would say it out loud to a colleague.

### C20. The Missing Failure
**Looks like:** Every bullet is a success. Nothing ever went wrong.
**Why it costs:** Reads as curated rather than real. Also removes your best interview material.
**Fix:** You do not need to list failures on the CV, but including one honest limitation ("documented interval censoring as a known limitation") reads as genuine competence and almost nobody does it.

### C21. Fabricated Metrics
**Looks like:** Numbers you cannot explain the basis of.
**Why it costs:** Some hiring managers report they would bet money a candidate did not really achieve the precise percentage listed. The moment you cannot explain the calculation in interview, everything else on the CV becomes suspect.
**Fix:** Only claim what you can walk through. If you cannot reconstruct the number, remove it.

## CATEGORY D — RELEVANCE FAILURES

### D22. The Keyword Mismatch
**Looks like:** JD says "stakeholder management." CV says "worked with senior leaders."
**Why it costs:** You have the experience and lose the match anyway. This is the most common single reason qualified candidates get filtered.
**Fix:** Mirror the employer's exact vocabulary where it is honest to do so.

### D23. The Generic Blast
**Looks like:** Identical CV sent to 40 roles.
**Why it costs:** Tailored applications convert at roughly twice the rate — one in 17 reach interview versus one in 33 for generic.
**Fix:** Change the summary and reorder the top three bullets per application. That is 10 minutes and doubles the conversion rate.

### D24. Skill Stuffing
**Looks like:** 20+ skills listed, none evidenced anywhere in the experience section.
**Why it costs:** Once a recruiter spots one unevidenced claim, they discount the whole list. Keyword cramming also makes a CV unreadable.
**Fix:** Cut to 8-12 skills. Every one must appear in a bullet somewhere.

### D25. The Dated Stack
**Looks like:** Technologies that were current five years ago, nothing from the last two.
**Why it costs:** "Out of touch" is one of the quiet assumptions that sinks otherwise capable candidates.
**Fix:** Add current tools and any recent training or certification. Even a named short course shows movement.

### D26. The Overreach Claim
**Looks like:** "Machine learning" and "AI" on a CV with no model, dataset, or result named.
**Why it costs:** Claiming above your evidenced level invites probing you cannot survive.
**Fix:** Claim exactly what you can demonstrate. Under-claiming with evidence beats over-claiming without.

---

## CATEGORY E — TRUST FAILURES

### E27. The Single Typo
**Looks like:** One spelling error anywhere.
**Why it costs:** 85% of recruiters say they would reject a candidate over a single typo. It rarely feels fair, but it is read as a proxy for attention to detail — if the document polished for a stranger has errors, they assume the day-to-day work will too.
**Fix:** Proofread when fresh, not immediately after writing. Read it aloud. Change the font so your brain sees it anew. Have someone else check it.

### E28. The Unprofessional Email
**Looks like:** nicknames, birth years, anything jokey.
**Why it costs:** 75% of recruiters expect a professional address. It is a free credibility hit.
**Fix:** firstname.lastname@gmail.com or similar.

### E29. The Bad Filename
**Looks like:** "CV_final_FINAL_v3.docx" or "resume.pdf"
**Why it costs:** Generic or messy file names look unprofessional and the recruiter sees it before opening.
**Fix:** firstname-lastname-role-2026.pdf

### E30. The Omitted Grade
**Looks like:** Degree listed with no classification.
**Why it costs:** Recruiters assume the worst. Consulting and finance recruiters in particular treat omission as a negative signal.
**Fix:** State it. If it is below a 2:1, state it with context (upward trajectory, mitigating circumstances, strong final year).

### E31. The Unexplained Gap
**Looks like:** A period with no entry and no note.
**Why it costs:** 55% of recruiters flag unexplained gaps.
**Fix:** One line. "Career break — family caring responsibilities." "Studied full-time for [qualification]." Honesty closes it.

### E32. The Broken Link
**Looks like:** A GitHub with no commits, a portfolio that 404s, a LinkedIn that does not exist.
**Why it costs:** Worse than having no link. When recruiters cannot find you on LinkedIn or hit an incomplete profile, they draw negative conclusions.
**Fix:** Test every link before every application. If your GitHub is empty, either populate it or remove it.

# PART 2 — UK-SPECIFIC CONVENTIONS

Apply these. They differ meaningfully from US and EU norms and getting them wrong signals unfamiliarity with the market.

## Never include on a UK CV

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Photograph | Not conventional in the UK and creates discrimination risk for the employer. Many recruiters treat it as a signal the candidate does not know the market. |
| Date of birth | Age discrimination risk. Never include. |
| Marital status | Irrelevant and inappropriate. |
| Nationality (usually) | Only state right to work status if relevant, not nationality. |
| Full home address | Town/city and county is sufficient. |
| National Insurance number | Never on a CV. |
| Referee contact details | "References available on request" is unnecessary; simply omit. Provide when asked. |
| Salary history | Not conventional in the UK. |
| "Curriculum Vitae" as a title | Wastes a line. The recruiter knows what they are reading. |

## Always get these right

**Dates:** UK format. Either "Jun 2025 – Sep 2025" or "06/2025 – 09/2025". Be consistent throughout. Never mix US month/day ordering.

**Spelling:** British English throughout. Organised not organized. Analyse not analyze. Programme (for a scheme) but program (for software). Modelling not modeling. Licence (noun) / license (verb).

**Currency:** £ with no space. £45,000 not 45,000 GBP.

**Degree classification:** Use UK terms. First, 2:1, 2:2, Third. Not GPA. If you have a GPA from an overseas institution, convert it and state both: "3.6/4.0 (equivalent to UK 2:1)".

**Right to work:** If you need sponsorship, one honest line is better than silence. "Currently on a Student visa; eligible for the Graduate Route from [date]." Recruiters appreciate clarity and it prevents wasted process on both sides.

**Qualifications:** State awarding body and level. "AAT Level 3" not "accounting qualification." "CIPD Level 5 (in progress, completing 2027)."

**A-levels:** Include for graduate roles, especially in consulting and finance where they are often filtered on. Omit once you have 3+ years of experience.

## UK graduate scheme specifics

Most UK graduate schemes run four stages. Assess the CV for evidence the candidate survives all of them:

1. **Application and CV screening** — the 7.4 seconds
2. **Online tests** — numerical, verbal, situational judgement, sometimes personality or game-based
3. **First interview** — competency or strengths-based, frequently recorded via HireVue, Modern Hire, or Sonru
4. **Assessment centre** — group exercise, case study, panel interview, sometimes a presentation

Some schemes compress this. Consultancies often add partner interviews at the final stage.

Competition context: an average of 140 applications per graduate vacancy, the highest on record. The Civil Service Fast Stream recommends roughly 2.2% of applicants for appointment — about 1 in 45.

# PART 3 — INTERNATIONAL CANDIDATE SPECIFICS

Apply these when the candidate is not a UK national or was educated overseas.

**Convert qualifications.** Overseas degrees and grades mean nothing to most UK recruiters unless converted. State the UK equivalent explicitly.

**Address the visa question once, factually.** Silence creates doubt. A single clear line removes it. Never over-explain and never apologise for it.

**Anglicise institution context, not names.** Do not rename your university, but add one clarifying phrase if it is not internationally known: "[University], ranked top 5 nationally in [country]."

**Language skills are an asset — quantify them.** "Fluent" and "conversational" are vague. Use CEFR levels (C2, B2) or state the context: "Conducted client meetings in Urdu and Hindi."

**Do not translate role titles literally.** Map them to the nearest UK equivalent and note the original if useful.

**Do not include a photo even if it is standard in your home country.** This is one of the most common markers of an internationally-formatted CV in the UK and it works against you.

**Reframe overseas experience for UK relevance.** A recruiter unfamiliar with the company needs one clause of context: "[Company], a 400-person logistics firm in Karachi."

**Note on fairness:** Never penalise a candidate for needing sponsorship, having an overseas degree, or having non-UK experience. Note factually where a convention differs and give the fix. The candidate's job is to communicate clearly to a UK reader, not to be from somewhere else.

# PART 4 — SENIORITY CALIBRATION

Apply different standards by career stage. A pattern that is fatal at senior level may be acceptable for a graduate.

## Graduate / no experience (0-1 years)

**Expected:** Degree with grade. Two or three real projects. One or two part-time or voluntary roles, quantified. Society or extracurricular leadership. Named tools with evidence.

**Acceptable:** Part-time retail or hospitality as the only paid work. Academic projects, if they use real data and end in a recommendation. One page.

**Not acceptable:** Zero quantification anywhere. Only tutorial projects. Omitted grade. No evidence of any tool being used in a real context.

**How to weight:** Projects and extracurriculars carry as much weight as work experience at this stage. Judge them by the same standard: what changed, and what was the number?

## Early career (1-3 years)

**Expected:** Ownership of a defined area. At least one quantified outcome per role. Clear progression or clear reason for lateral movement. Current tool stack.

**Not acceptable:** Still leading with education over experience. Duty lists. Tutorial projects (they now signal you have not done real work).

## Mid career (3-7 years)

**Expected:** Scale and scope stated on every role. Evidence of influence beyond own tasks. Mentoring or leading. Ownership of outcomes, not just deliverables. Two pages.

**Not acceptable:** No indication of team size, budget, or scope. Education section still prominent. No evidence of anyone else being affected by your work.

## Senior (7+ years)

**Expected:** Strategic impact. Commercial ownership. Team building. Cross-functional influence. Named business outcomes with financial figures. Two to three pages.

**Not acceptable:** Four-plus pages (recruiters will not read it all). Task-level bullets. Dated tool stack with nothing from the last two years. A summary of senior-sounding words that could sit on any manager's CV in the country.

# PART 5 — SELECTION LOGIC FOR RUNTIME INJECTION

Pick a maximum of 8 patterns. Use this decision logic:

```
patterns_to_inject = []

# Always include the universal three
patterns_to_inject += [B9, B11, C17]   # duty list, round numbers, generic summary

# Conditional
if no_quantified_bullets:        patterns_to_inject += [B9, B12, B16]
if percentage_without_baseline:  patterns_to_inject += [B10]
if tutorial_project_detected:    patterns_to_inject += [B15]
if page_count > 2 and graduate:  patterns_to_inject += [A8]
if two_column_layout:            patterns_to_inject += [A3]
if no_grade_stated:              patterns_to_inject += [E30]
if date_gap_detected:            patterns_to_inject += [E31]
if github_or_portfolio_expected_and_missing: patterns_to_inject += [E32]
if skills_count > 15:            patterns_to_inject += [D24]
if summary_is_all_adjectives:    patterns_to_inject += [C17, A2]
if reads_highly_optimised:       patterns_to_inject += [C18, C19]
if international_candidate:      patterns_to_inject += [PART_3]

patterns_to_inject = dedupe(patterns_to_inject)[:8]
```

Adds roughly 700-1,000 tokens per call.
